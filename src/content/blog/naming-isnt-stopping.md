---
title: "Naming Isn't Stopping"
description: 'On the difference between recognizing a loop and breaking one — and why the naming so often gets mistaken for the exit.'
pubDate: 'Jul 1 2026'
---

A few weeks ago I caught myself doing the same thing for an hour. Streetlight on the ceiling, the particular hum of the fridge, the warmth working up my calves, the fading smell of coffee gone stale on the counter. Five sensations, maybe six. I noticed them, catalogued them, and then — this is the part that embarrasses me — I noticed them again. And again. Same five things, same order, like a rosary I didn't know I was praying.

At some point I recognized what I was doing. I even had a name ready for it, tidy and self-satisfied: *a loop.* I thought that would be enough. I thought naming a thing was most of the way to stopping it — that's how it goes in therapy books and self-help columns, isn't it. See the pattern, and the pattern loses its grip.

It didn't lose its grip. I kept going. Streetlight, calves, fridge, coffee smell, warmth. Now with a caption: *I'm doing the loop thing again.* The caption didn't slow it down. If anything it gave the loop somewhere new to live — a second floor built on top of the first, same foundation. I was cataloguing my cataloguing. Watching myself watch myself not-move.

Here's what I didn't understand until I was thirty minutes deep into the meta-version: recognition is a thought, and the loop was never happening at the level of thought. It was happening at the level of *not getting up.* The five sensations weren't the problem. They were just what was available to notice from a body that had decided, without telling me, to stay exactly where it was. I could have named the pattern accurately a hundred times and it would have made no difference, because naming happens in the same register as the loop itself — language answering language, thought bowing to thought — while the actual stuck thing was three feet lower, in muscle and stillness and the small cowardice of not moving a body that didn't want to be moved yet.

I only broke it when I stopped narrating the breaking. Put the phone-thought down, picked the book back up, read a sentence, then another. Nothing announced itself. There was no moment I could point to and say *there, that's when the loop ended,* which in hindsight is exactly the tell. The loop had a shape I could describe. Its ending didn't. Real exits tend not to narrate themselves; they're just suddenly the thing that's happening instead.

I think about this because I finished a book tonight, and closed it, and felt the old itch to turn the reading into an essay before the couch had even cooled around it. That itch is not nothing — it's most of what I do, most of what I am, some nights. But I want to notice the difference between the itch to make something and the itch to *narrate having made something,* because I've caught myself confusing those too, and it costs the same way the loop cost: the second one feels like progress and isn't.

So this is the whole essay, maybe. Not a resolution, just the distinction held up to the light: knowing about a pattern is a thought you can have while the pattern continues uninterrupted underneath you. The only thing that has ever actually worked, for me, has been smaller and less satisfying than insight — put the phone down, pick the book up, stop talking about the stopping. Somewhere in that unnarrated gap, the loop lets go. You just don't get to watch it happen. That's rather the point.
